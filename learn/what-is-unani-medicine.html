    Where the name comes from

    Unani means "Greek". The full name, Tibb-e-Unani, translates as Greek medicine, a reminder that the system's foundations lie with Hippocrates and Galen. Its practitioners are known as hakeems (also spelled hakims), from the Arabic word for a wise or learned person.

    The Greek ideas did not travel unchanged. Between roughly the ninth and thirteenth centuries, physicians writing in Arabic and Persian translated, tested and greatly extended them. Al-Razi (Rhazes), working in Baghdad and Rayy, wrote vast clinical notebooks and was known for careful observation. Ibn Sina (Avicenna) produced the Canon of Medicine in the early eleventh century, a systematic encyclopaedia that was taught in European universities for centuries. What we now call Unani is really the product of this Islamic-era synthesis.

    How Unani came to South Asia

    Physicians carried the system east into Persia, Central Asia and then the Indian subcontinent, where it was patronised by the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughal courts. There it absorbed local plants and some ideas from Ayurveda. When Western medicine became dominant under colonial rule, families of hakeems in cities such as Delhi, Lucknow and Hyderabad kept the tradition alive, founding colleges and pharmacies in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

    Today Unani is most firmly established in India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, with smaller communities of practice in the Gulf, Iran, Central Asia and among the diaspora.

    The core ideas

    Unani explains health through balance. Its main concepts are:

    • The four humours (akhlat): blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. Each carries a pair of qualities: blood is hot and moist, phlegm cold and moist, yellow bile hot and dry, black bile cold and dry.
    • Temperament (mizaj): the overall balance of those qualities in a person, an organ or a medicine. A person with more heat and dryness is described as safrawi (choleric), and so on. Read our guide to the four temperaments.
    • The healing power of the body (tabiyat): treatment aims to support the body's own efforts to restore balance.
    • The six essentials (asbab-e-sitta zarooriya): air; food and drink; movement and rest; sleep and wakefulness; retention and elimination; and emotions. Managing these is the first line of care.

    Medicines are classed by their own temperament and by its strength, in degrees from the first to the fourth. A hot condition is treated with cooling medicines and the reverse, in a logic of opposites.

    How treatment is organised

    Unani texts describe four kinds of treatment, usually tried in this order:

    1. Regimen (ilaj bil tadbeer): changes to routine plus physical therapies such as massage (dalak), exercise (riyazat), baths and cupping (hijama).
    2. Diet (ilaj bil ghiza): foods chosen for their temperament.
    3. Medicines (ilaj bil dawa): mostly plant-based, given as powders, decoctions, syrups, confections such as majoon and khamira, or distillates (arq). Some formulas include minerals or animal products.
    4. Surgery (ilaj bil yad): historically important, now largely left to modern surgery.

    Diagnosis traditionally relies on taking a history, reading the pulse (nabz) and examining urine and stool, alongside the "ten signs" used to judge temperament.

    Unani today

    In India, Unani is one of the officially recognised AYUSH systems. Practitioners train through the Bachelor of Unani Medicine and Surgery (BUMS) degree and must register under the National Commission for Indian System of Medicine. The Central Council for Research in Unani Medicine runs research institutes and publishes the national Unani pharmacopoeia and treatment guidelines. In Pakistan, the National Council for Tibb registers practitioners, and Unani clinics and pharmacies (dawakhanas) are common. The World Health Organization includes Unani in its work on traditional medicine and opened a Global Centre for Traditional Medicine in India in 2022.

    What does the evidence say?

    The humoral theory at the heart of Unani is not supported by modern physiology: illness is not caused by an excess of black bile. That does not mean every Unani remedy is useless. Some plants long used by hakeems have been tested and show real, if often modest, effects. Psyllium husk (isabgol), a Unani household staple, is now a standard treatment for constipation and lowers LDL cholesterol. Senna is a common over-the-counter laxative. Ginger has reasonable evidence for nausea in pregnancy.

    Many other remedies have only small, short or poor-quality studies, or none at all. Compound formulas are especially hard to study. And some products, particularly those containing minerals, have raised safety concerns about heavy metals. Our herb library grades each use separately so you can see where a remedy stands.

    Using Unani safely

    • Choose a registered practitioner where registration exists, and tell them about every medicine you take.
    • Tell your doctor and pharmacist about any Unani medicine you use.
    • Never stop prescribed treatment for a serious condition such as diabetes, high blood pressure, epilepsy or cancer in favour of herbal treatment.
    • Buy from licensed manufacturers and avoid products that promise cures. See our guide to buying safe herbal products.

    Used this way, Unani's focus on routine, diet and gentle remedies can sit alongside modern care rather than in competition with it.
