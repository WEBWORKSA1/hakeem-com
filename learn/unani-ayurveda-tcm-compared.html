    Three systems, shared instincts

    Unani, Ayurveda and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) developed in different places, but they share a striking set of instincts: health is balance, the individual's constitution matters, daily routine and diet come before drugs, and plants are the main medicines. They also differ in vocabulary and theory, and the same herb can be described quite differently in each.

    The same plant, three descriptions

    Take ginger. In Unani it is hot and dry, used to warm the stomach and clear phlegm. In Ayurveda, dried ginger is pungent and heating, prized as a digestive and called a universal medicine. In TCM, fresh ginger is used for nausea and the first signs of a cold, while dried ginger is used to warm the body's interior. The theories differ, but the practical uses overlap remarkably, and nausea is the use with the best modern evidence.

    Training and regulation

    • Unani: in India, the BUMS degree and registration with the National Commission for Indian System of Medicine; in Pakistan, registration with the National Council for Tibb.
    • Ayurveda: in India, the BAMS degree under the same national commission; elsewhere, practice is often unregulated or regulated as a general wellness service.
    • Chinese medicine: regulated as a profession in China and in several other places, including some Canadian provinces, Australia and many US states for acupuncture.

    Outside their home countries, all three are unevenly regulated. Ask any practitioner what qualification they hold and who regulates it.

    What the evidence shows across all three

    The underlying theories (humours, doshas and qi) are not accepted by modern physiology. But each tradition has given rise to remedies that work. Artemisinin, now a front-line malaria drug, was rediscovered from a classical Chinese text. Psyllium, staple of Unani and Ayurvedic home care, is now standard fibre therapy. Many other remedies remain untested or show small effects, and quality problems such as heavy-metal contamination affect products from all three traditions.

    Choosing between them

    For most people the choice comes down to culture, access and the practitioner more than the system. Look for a qualified, registered practitioner who is open about evidence, asks about your medicines, and is happy to work alongside your doctor. You can request an introduction through Hakeem.com.
