    What hijama is

    Hijama is the Arabic name for cupping, one of the regimenal therapies (ilaj bil tadbeer) of Unani medicine. Cups are placed on the skin and a vacuum is created, either with a hand pump or with heat, so that the skin and underlying tissue are drawn up into the cup.

    • Dry cupping uses suction alone, usually for a few minutes.
    • Wet cupping, the classic form of hijama, adds small, shallow cuts to the skin before the cups are reapplied to draw out a small amount of blood.

    Cupping is also part of Chinese medicine and of folk medicine in many cultures. In Muslim communities, hijama has special standing because it is recommended in prophetic medicine tradition, and some people schedule it on particular days of the lunar month.

    What the research shows

    Most cupping research focuses on pain, especially neck pain, lower back pain and fibromyalgia. Reviews of these trials suggest cupping may reduce pain in the short term compared with no treatment. However, the trials are generally small, often at high risk of bias, and it is very difficult to create a convincing "fake" cupping for comparison, so placebo effects are hard to rule out. There is little reliable evidence for claims that cupping removes toxins, treats high blood pressure, diabetes, infertility or other internal diseases.

    In short: hijama may help some people with musculoskeletal pain, and its benefits for other conditions are unproven.

    The risks

    • Bruising and circular marks are expected and usually fade within one to two weeks.
    • Burns from heated cups.
    • Infection of the cuts if hygiene is poor, and in rare cases scarring or keloids.
    • Blood-borne infections such as hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV if blades or cups are reused or not properly sterilised.
    • Fainting or anaemia, especially with repeated wet cupping.

    Who should avoid it or ask a doctor first

    • People taking blood thinners or with bleeding disorders
    • Pregnant women
    • People with anaemia, diabetes with poor wound healing, or a weakened immune system
    • Anyone with skin infections, eczema or broken skin where the cups would go
    • People with a heart device or serious heart, kidney or liver disease

    Questions to ask a hijama practitioner

    1. What training do you have, and are you registered with any regulator?
    2. Are blades single-use and opened in front of me?
    3. Are cups disposable, or how are they sterilised?
    4. Do you wear fresh gloves and clean the skin before and after?
    5. Will you ask about my medicines and health conditions first?
    6. What aftercare do you advise, and what should prompt me to see a doctor?

    A good practitioner will welcome these questions. If the answers are vague, go elsewhere.

    After a session

    Keep any cuts clean and dry, and watch for spreading redness, warmth, pus or fever, which are signs of infection that need medical care. Stay hydrated and rest if you feel light-headed.
