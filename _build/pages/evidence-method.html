How we grade evidence

Each claimed use of a herb gets its own grade, because a herb can be well proven for one thing and useless for another. Psyllium for constipation and ginkgo for memory sit at opposite ends of the same scale.

{{block:grade_scale}}

What each grade requires

Strong. Consistent results from several well-conducted randomised controlled trials in people, usually confirmed by a systematic review, or an official regulatory acceptance for that use. Example: psyllium for lowering LDL cholesterol.

Moderate. Several human trials point the same way, but they are small, short or uneven in quality, so a future large trial could change the picture. Example: ginger for nausea in pregnancy.

Limited. A few small human studies, or results that conflict, or mainly laboratory and animal evidence with some human data. Example: black seed for blood pressure.

Insufficient. Too little reliable human research to judge. This does not mean the herb fails; it means nobody has tested it properly.

Not supported. Good-quality trials have looked and found no benefit. Example: ginkgo for preventing dementia.

What we weigh

  • Human trials over lab studies. An effect in a test tube or in mice is a reason to study a herb, not proof that it works in people.
  • Size and length. Twenty people for four weeks tells us much less than a thousand people for a year.
  • Consistency. One positive trial among several negative ones is not a positive result.
  • Independence. We note when most trials are funded by a product's manufacturer.
  • The product tested. A standardised extract in a trial may behave very differently from a tea or powder bought at a market.

Where traditional use fits

Traditional use is valuable: it is how many modern medicines were found, from aspirin's origins in willow bark to artemisinin for malaria. But a long history of use shows that people found a remedy worth using, not that it works better than placebo or is free of harm. That is why every profile shows tradition and evidence in separate sections, and why temperament classifications appear without an evidence grade.

Safety is graded separately

A herb with weak evidence of benefit can still carry strong evidence of harm. Our safety sections list known side effects, who should avoid the herb and documented interactions, whatever the evidence for benefit.
