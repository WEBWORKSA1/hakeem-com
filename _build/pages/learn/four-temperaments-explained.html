One idea, two axes

Unani medicine describes everything in the body, and everything that goes into it, by four primary qualities: hot, cold, moist and dry. The particular mix of these qualities in a person is their mizaj, or temperament. The same scheme is applied to organs, foods, medicines, seasons and even ages of life.

It helps to picture two axes. One runs from cold to hot, the other from moist to dry. Every person or herb can be placed somewhere on that map, which is exactly what our temperament map and mizaj quiz do.

The four humours

The qualities are carried by four humours (akhlat), each linked to a classical temperament:

  • Dam (blood): hot and moist. When dominant, the temperament is damwi, or sanguine.
  • Balgham (phlegm): cold and moist. Balghami, or phlegmatic.
  • Safra (yellow bile): hot and dry. Safrawi, or choleric.
  • Sauda (black bile): cold and dry. Saudawi, or melancholic.

English still carries traces of this scheme: we call a cheerful person sanguine and a gloomy one melancholic.

Nine temperaments, not four

Classical texts actually describe nine temperaments. One is balanced (mu'tadil). Four are "simple", where one quality dominates: hot, cold, moist or dry. Four are "compound", combining two qualities: hot and moist, hot and dry, cold and moist, cold and dry. The compound ones correspond to the four humours above and are the ones most people mean when they talk about temperament.

Unani also distinguishes a person's natural, lifelong temperament from their current state. An imbalance, su-e-mizaj, is the classical explanation of illness: a hot-tempered person might become "too hot" in summer, for example.

How a practitioner assesses temperament

Practitioners use the "ten signs" (ajnas-e-ashra), which include:

  • how the skin feels to the touch (warm or cool, soft or dry)
  • build and the balance of muscle and fat
  • hair: thickness, texture and growth
  • response to heat and cold
  • sleep and wakefulness
  • the functioning of organs, including appetite and digestion
  • excretions
  • emotional disposition

Pulse reading adds further information in a clinical setting. Our quiz uses simplified versions of these signs, leaving out anything a questionnaire cannot judge.

Keeping balance: the six essentials

Because temperament is shaped by daily life, Unani's first tool for keeping balance is the six essentials: air, food and drink, movement and rest, sleep and wakefulness, retention and elimination, and emotions. A person described as hot and dry might be advised cooling, moistening foods, moderate exercise in cool hours and ways to manage anger. Someone cold and moist might be advised warm, lightly spiced food, brisk activity and early rising.

Much of this advice overlaps with sensible general guidance on sleep, activity and diet, which is one reason people find it useful.

Temperament of medicines

Each medicine is also given a temperament and a strength, in degrees from first to fourth. Garlic and clove are considered strongly hot and dry; psyllium is cold and moist; licorice is close to balanced. Treatment follows the logic of opposites: cooling remedies for heat, warming remedies for cold. Texts sometimes disagree about a plant's classification, which is why our map positions are approximate.

What modern science says

Modern physiology does not recognise the four humours, and temperament has not been shown to predict disease or response to treatment in rigorous studies. Researchers, including at India's Central Council for Research in Unani Medicine, have tried to standardise temperament assessment, and some studies look for links with measurable traits such as body composition or metabolic rate. These are early efforts.

The honest summary: temperament is a coherent traditional framework for describing differences between people, and its lifestyle advice is often reasonable. It is not a diagnostic test, and it should never replace investigation of new or serious symptoms.
