The kitchen pharmacy

Long before supplements came in capsules, home remedies came from the spice box. Many families still reach for haldi doodh (turmeric milk) for a cold, ajwain or saunf for an upset stomach, or kalonji with honey for general health. These spices are safe in cooking amounts for nearly everyone. The questions are whether they help as remedies, and whether concentrated supplements change the safety picture.

Food amounts versus supplements

A teaspoon of turmeric in a curry delivers a small amount of curcumin, most of which is poorly absorbed. A high-absorption curcumin supplement can deliver many times more into the bloodstream, and that is where rare liver injuries have been reported. The same pattern holds for green tea, where the drink is safe but concentrated extracts have been linked to liver problems. As a rule, the more concentrated and absorbable the product, the more it behaves like a drug, for better and worse.

Where the hype runs ahead

Turmeric is the clearest example. Lab studies show curcumin affecting inflammation and cancer pathways, and these results are often repeated as if they were proven benefits in people. Human trials are much less dramatic: modest pain relief in knee osteoarthritis in short studies, and little solid evidence for most other claims. Cinnamon for diabetes follows a similar pattern of early excitement and mixed trials.

A sensible approach

  • Cook with these spices freely; they make healthy food enjoyable.
  • Treat capsules and extracts as medicines: check interactions and tell your pharmacist.
  • Do not use spices to replace treatment for diabetes, high blood pressure or other conditions.
  • Buy whole spices or tested brands to avoid adulteration.
