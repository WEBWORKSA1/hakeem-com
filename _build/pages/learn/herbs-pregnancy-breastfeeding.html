The starting principle

Pregnancy is the time when herbal safety matters most and is known least. For ethical reasons, very few herbs have been tested in pregnant women, so for most remedies there is simply no reliable safety data. The sensible approach is to treat every herbal medicine as a medicine: use it only when there is a clear reason, and only after checking with your midwife, doctor or pharmacist.

Herbs and spices used in normal cooking amounts are a different matter. A pinch of turmeric in dal or fennel seeds after a meal is not the same as concentrated capsules.

Ginger: the best-studied option

Ginger is the one herbal remedy with reasonable evidence in pregnancy. Several trials show that ginger reduces nausea in early pregnancy compared with placebo, and it appears in some professional guidance as an option for mild nausea. Studies generally used modest amounts of dried ginger. Ask your midwife or doctor before starting, especially if you have a bleeding disorder or take medicines that affect clotting.

Herbs to avoid or use only on advice

  • Licorice root: heavy use in pregnancy has been linked to earlier birth; it can also raise blood pressure.
  • Ashwagandha: not recommended in pregnancy.
  • Fenugreek in medicinal doses: may stimulate the uterus.
  • Neem: avoid in pregnancy and when trying to conceive.
  • Aloe latex and strong laxatives: avoid oral aloe; use senna only on medical advice.
  • Saffron above food amounts: high doses can stimulate the uterus.
  • Moringa root, bark and flowers: avoid; leaves as food are a different matter.
  • St. John's wort: not recommended, and it interacts with many medicines.
  • Concentrated essential oils taken by mouth, including fennel oil and clove oil.

This list is not complete. If a herb is not listed, that does not mean it is safe.

Breastfeeding

Many substances pass into breast milk. Herbs promoted to increase milk supply, such as fenugreek and moringa, have weak evidence, and fenugreek can cause stomach upset in mothers and babies and a maple-syrup smell that may confuse doctors. The most effective ways to increase supply are frequent feeding or pumping and support from a lactation consultant. Before taking a galactagogue herb, talk to your doctor or a lactation specialist.

Herbs and babies

Babies are especially vulnerable. Never give herbal oils such as neem oil or clove oil to an infant, do not apply mint or menthol products near a baby's face, and ask a doctor before giving any herbal preparation, including gripe waters, to a baby.

Questions to ask your midwife or doctor

  1. Is there evidence this herb is safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding?
  2. Is there a better-studied option for my symptom?
  3. Could it interact with my vitamins or medicines?
  4. What warning signs should make me stop?

When to get help urgently

In pregnancy, seek medical help straight away for vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, severe headache, vision changes, reduced baby movements, or vomiting so severe you cannot keep fluids down. Do not treat these with herbs.
