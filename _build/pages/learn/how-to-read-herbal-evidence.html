The evidence ladder

Not all research is equal. From weakest to strongest, the main kinds of evidence are:

  1. Tradition and anecdote. A remedy has been used for centuries, or someone felt better after taking it. Valuable for finding leads; weak as proof, because people often recover on their own.
  2. Laboratory studies. An extract kills cancer cells in a dish or reduces inflammation markers in cells. Many substances do this, including ones that are useless or harmful in people.
  3. Animal studies. Effects in mice or rats, usually at doses far higher than people take.
  4. Observational studies in people. People who drink more tea have fewer heart attacks, for example. These show links, not cause and effect, because tea drinkers may differ in many other ways.
  5. Randomised controlled trials (RCTs). People are randomly assigned to the herb or a placebo, ideally without anyone knowing who got what. This is the best way to test whether something works.
  6. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Researchers gather all the trials on a question and combine the results. Strong when the underlying trials are good; misleading when they are not.

Common traps

Small and short trials

Many herbal trials enrol a few dozen people for a few weeks. Small trials are more likely to produce exaggerated results by chance, and short trials cannot show long-term benefit or rare harms.

Surrogate outcomes

A herb may lower a blood marker without improving what matters to you, such as fewer heart attacks or less pain. A change in a lab number is a good start, not the finish line.

The product problem

Trials usually test a specific, standardised extract. The tea, powder or capsule you buy may contain very different amounts of the active compounds.

Who paid?

Industry funding does not make a trial wrong, but trials funded by a product's maker are more likely to report favourable results. Look for independent replication.

Publication bias

Positive trials are more likely to be published than negative ones, so the published record can look better than reality.

Placebo effects

For symptoms like pain, fatigue, mood and sleep, people often improve when they expect to. That is why a placebo comparison is essential before concluding that a herb works.

Five questions to ask about any claim

  1. Was it tested in people, or only in cells or animals?
  2. Was there a comparison group, ideally a placebo, and were people randomly assigned?
  3. How many people took part, and for how long?
  4. Has anyone else found the same result?
  5. Was the product tested the same as the one being sold to you?

Reading health headlines

Be sceptical of headlines that say a herb "cures", "reverses" or "destroys" a disease. Check whether the study was in people, and look for words like "may", "linked to" and "in mice" that often get lost between the study and the headline.

Evidence of harm counts too

Safety evidence often comes from case reports: individual patients who developed liver injury after a supplement, for example. A handful of reports does not prove a herb caused the problem, but regulators and databases such as the US National Library of Medicine's LiverTox take patterns of reports seriously. We include these signals in our safety sections even when the evidence of benefit is weak.
