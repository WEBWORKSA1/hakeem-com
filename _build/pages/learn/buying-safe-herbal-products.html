Why quality is a real problem

Unlike prescription medicines, most herbal products are not tested by regulators for effectiveness before sale, and quality varies widely. Problems found in studies and safety alerts include:

  • Heavy metals. A study published in JAMA in 2008 tested Ayurvedic medicines bought online and found that about one in five contained detectable lead, mercury or arsenic. Some traditional formulas deliberately include metals or minerals, while other contamination comes from soil or processing.
  • Adulteration. Expensive herbs such as saffron are often bulked out or faked, and turmeric powder has been found coloured with lead chromate.
  • Undeclared drugs. Some products sold as herbal for weight loss, sexual performance or joint pain have been found to contain hidden prescription drugs such as steroids or sildenafil.
  • Wrong plant or wrong dose. The plant on the label may not be the plant in the bottle, or the amount may differ greatly from what is claimed.

How regulation differs

  • United States. Herbal products are mostly sold as dietary supplements. Manufacturers must follow good manufacturing practice, but the FDA does not approve supplements before sale, and they may not claim to treat disease.
  • Canada. Natural health products need a licence before sale and carry an eight-digit Natural Product Number (NPN) on the label.
  • European Union and United Kingdom. Herbal medicines can be registered under a traditional-use scheme that requires evidence of long use and quality standards; in the UK, registered products carry a "THR" mark.
  • India. Unani, Ayurvedic and Siddha medicines need a manufacturing licence from state licensing authorities under the Ministry of AYUSH framework, and the licence number should appear on the pack.
  • Pakistan. Herbal and Unani products are regulated by the Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan and should carry a registration or enlistment number.

Seven checks before you buy

  1. Read the Latin name. It should match the plant you want, and the plant part (root, leaf, seed) should be stated.
  2. Look for a licence or registration number appropriate to your country: NPN, THR, AYUSH manufacturing licence or DRAP number.
  3. Look for independent testing. Seals from programmes such as USP Verified or NSF, or a published certificate of analysis for heavy metals and microbes, are good signs.
  4. Check the dose. Avoid "proprietary blends" that hide how much of each herb you are getting.
  5. Be wary of mineral-containing formulas (bhasmas and kushtas) unless prescribed by a qualified practitioner who explains the testing.
  6. Buy from established pharmacies or the manufacturer, not anonymous marketplace sellers with unusually low prices.
  7. Keep the packaging so you can report a problem and identify the batch.

Red flags

  • Claims to cure diabetes, cancer, infertility, kidney disease or other serious conditions.
  • "Works instantly" or "100% safe, no side effects" promises.
  • Products for weight loss, bodybuilding or sexual performance that act dramatically fast; these are the categories most often found to contain hidden drugs.
  • No manufacturer address, batch number or expiry date.
  • Labels only in a language you cannot read, with no information in yours.

Loose herbs and kitchen spices

Whole, recognisable herbs and spices, such as fennel seeds, cloves or cinnamon sticks, are harder to adulterate than powders. Buy whole where you can and grind at home. For powders, choose brands that publish testing results.

If something goes wrong

Stop the product, keep the packaging and tell your doctor or pharmacist. Report suspected side effects to your national regulator, such as MedWatch in the US, MedEffect in Canada, the Yellow Card scheme in the UK, or India's pharmacovigilance programme for ASU&H drugs.
