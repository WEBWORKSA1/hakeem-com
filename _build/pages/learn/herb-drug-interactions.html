Why "natural" does not mean "no interactions"

Herbs contain active chemicals. That is the whole point of taking them, and it is also why they can interact with medicines. Surveys in many countries find that a large share of people who use herbal products also take prescription drugs, and many never mention the herbs to their doctor or pharmacist.

Most interactions are minor. A few are serious enough to cause bleeding, transplant rejection, an unplanned pregnancy or dangerously low blood sugar.

The three ways interactions happen

1. Changing how the body clears a drug

The liver uses enzymes, especially a family called cytochrome P450, and transport proteins to break down and remove many medicines. Some herbs speed these systems up, so the drug is cleared faster and its level falls. Others slow them down, so the level rises. St. John's wort is the classic example: it speeds up clearance of many drugs, including the contraceptive pill, the anticoagulant warfarin, the transplant drug cyclosporine and several HIV medicines.

2. Adding to a drug's effect

Some herbs act in the same direction as a medicine. Garlic, ginkgo, high-dose turmeric and clove can slow blood clotting, adding to blood thinners. Fenugreek, black seed and cinnamon may lower blood sugar, adding to diabetes medicines. Ashwagandha and chamomile can add to the drowsiness caused by sedatives. Licorice works in the opposite direction from blood pressure medicines by raising blood pressure.

3. Interfering with absorption

Fibre such as psyllium husk (isabgol) can bind medicines in the gut if taken at the same time, reducing how much is absorbed. Taking them at least two hours apart usually solves this.

Who is most at risk

  • People taking blood thinners, diabetes medicines, heart-rhythm drugs, transplant or immune-suppressing drugs, HIV medicines, lithium, anti-epileptic drugs or chemotherapy.
  • Older adults taking several medicines.
  • Anyone having surgery, because of bleeding and anaesthetic risks.
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women.
  • People with liver or kidney disease, which affects how both herbs and drugs are cleared.

Four habits that prevent most problems

  1. Keep a list. Write down every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, vitamin, supplement and regular herbal tea. Include doses.
  2. Show it. Take the list to every appointment and to your pharmacist whenever a new medicine is started. Pharmacists are often the quickest source of interaction advice.
  3. Pause before surgery. Stop herbal supplements about two weeks before planned surgery or dental surgery, unless your surgeon advises otherwise.
  4. Change one thing at a time. Start one new product at a time and watch for changes, so you know what caused any effect.

Warning signs to act on

Contact a doctor promptly if, after starting a herb, you notice unusual bruising or bleeding, shakiness and sweating (possible low blood sugar), yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, palpitations, severe drowsiness, or a change in how well a medicine seems to work.

The bottom line

Herbs and medicines can be used together safely in many cases, but only when the people prescribing and dispensing your medicines know what you take. If in doubt, ask before you add anything.
