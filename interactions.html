Why herbs interact with medicines

Most interactions happen in one of three ways. Some herbs speed up or slow down the liver enzymes that clear medicines from the body, which changes how much of the drug stays in your blood. St. John's wort is the best-known example: it can lower the levels of birth control pills, transplant drugs and some HIV medicines enough to make them fail.

Other herbs add to a medicine's effect. Garlic, ginkgo and high-dose turmeric can slow clotting, so they add to the effect of blood thinners. Fenugreek and black seed can lower blood sugar, which adds to diabetes medicines. The third route is absorption: fibre such as psyllium can bind medicines in the gut if they are taken at the same time.

Four habits that prevent most problems

  1. Keep a written list of every medicine, vitamin and herbal product you take, including teas taken daily.
  2. Show it to your pharmacist whenever a new medicine is started.
  3. Stop herbal supplements two weeks before planned surgery unless your surgeon says otherwise.
  4. Start one new product at a time, so you can tell what caused any change.
